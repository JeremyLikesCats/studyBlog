## Sound waves

Sound waves can be represented in a few different ways. Of these few, the ways presented in the book is pressure, density and displacement. We use the bulk modulus in relation to the pressure.

:::tip[The Bulk Modulus]
The bulk modulus is defined as:
$$
B = -V \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta p}
$$
:::

:::note[1. Pressure and Density]
For pressure as a sinusoidal wave:
$$p (x, t) = \Delta p_{m} \sin(kx - \omega t).$$Similarly, for density as a sinusoidal wave:
$$
	\rho(x,t) = \Delta \rho_{m} \sin(kx-\omega t).
$$
:::

:::note[2. Displacement]
For displacement as a sinusoidal wave:
$$
s = \frac{\Delta p_{m}}{kB}\sin(kx-\omega t) = \frac{\Delta \rho_{m}}{k\rho_{0}} \sin(kx-\omega t).
$$
Or more generally,
$$
s = \frac{p(x,t)}{kB} = \frac{\rho(x,t)}{k\rho_{0}}.
$$
:::

I also learned about the speed of a longitudinal wave in a medium.

:::note[3. Speed of sound]
The speed of a longitudinal wave can be expressed as:
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}
$$
:::

Woo! Simple enough! But then comes the parts that I struggle to memorize.

:::note[4. Power]
We know that power can be expressed as $P = Fv$. Using this definition, we can do the following derivation:
$$
F = pA = \Delta p_m \sin(kx-\omega t) A, v = \frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{\Delta p_m \omega}{kB} \sin(kx - \omega t)
$$
$$
\implies P = \frac{A \omega (\Delta p_m)^2}{kB} \sin^2(kx-\omega t) = \frac{Av (\Delta p_m)^2}{B} \sin^2(kx-\omega t).
$$
From this we can obtain an expression for $P_{avg}$. We use the fact that the average of $\sin^2(x)$ for some random x is $\frac{1}{2}$. Then, we get:
$$
P_{avg} = \frac{Av (\Delta p_m)^2}{2B}.
$$
:::tip[Intensity]
It is useful to define a value called Intensity as (Average Pressure)/(Area). This logarithmically links with how our ears react to sound.
$$
I = \frac{P_av}{A} = \frac{v (\Delta p_m)^2}{2B}.
$$
Now, we can define a quantity called the *sound level* SL, measured in decibels (dB) (Hmm I think I've seen this unit many times before...), which is defined as:
$$
SL = 10 \log{\frac{I}{I_0}}
$$
where $I_0 = 10^{-12} W/m^2$.

:::
Finally, I learned about interference, and how it is important that we use pressure to represent sound because of this.

:::note[5. Interference]
The *phase difference* can be expressed as, for two waves with the same wavelength:
$$
\Delta \phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Delta L.
$$

When the pressure is in phase, they constructively interfere. When they are out of phase by $\pi$, they destructively interfere.
:::
